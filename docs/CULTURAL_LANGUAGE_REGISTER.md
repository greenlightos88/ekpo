# EKPO — CULTURAL LANGUAGE REGISTER

Status: ACTIVE SCREENPLAY CONTROL DOCUMENT
Effective: 2026-08-13
Scope: Nigerian English, Nigerian Pidgin, Efik, code-switching, household speech, diaspora/local contrast

This document supplements `docs/CHARACTER_VOICE_CONSTITUTION.md`.

It does not replace cultural consultation. It establishes a disciplined screenplay rule so local Nigerian characters do not default to flattened North American dialogue while also preventing caricature, fake accent writing, or random Pidgin seasoning.

## Foundational rule

Nigerian Pidgin is not `broken English` and must never be written as misspelled Standard English to simulate an accent.

Nigerian English, Nigerian Pidgin, Efik, and Standard/International English are distinct available registers.

Characters may code-switch according to:

- who they are speaking to;
- age/status relationship;
- intimacy;
- public versus private setting;
- humor;
- anger;
- urgency;
- whether they are accommodating Canadian-raised family members;
- whether they want an outsider to understand everything being said.

Language choice is character action.

Do not sprinkle Pidgin merely to remind the audience the scene is in Nigeria.

## Diaspora / local contrast

### Malik, Ade, Kai

Raised in Vancouver.

Their principal dialogue remains Canadian/North American English shaped by their individual voice systems.

They may understand or acquire common Nigerian English/Pidgin expressions through family/context, but they should not suddenly speak fluent Pidgin for local color.

Their Efik use should reflect actual established familiarity, family greeting, learned phrases, or later development — not invented fluency.

### Bode

Raised in Nigeria and culturally local.

Bode should not sound American even when speaking grammatically standard English.

His available registers:

1. **Nigerian English** — default with nephews when clarity matters, formal family discussion, serious explanation.
2. **Nigerian Pidgin** — local peer interaction, informal negotiation, irritation, reassurance, humor, practical urgency, solidarity.
3. **Efik** — greeting, intimacy, kinship, elder/family connection, emotionally private moments where appropriate and verified.

Bode often accommodates the Canadian-raised nephews by moving toward clearer English. That accommodation itself should be visible: he may speak one register to a porter/Eyo and another to Malik in the same scene.

Under fear or anger, he should become **more local and more compressed**, not more eloquent.

Do not make Bode a walking Pidgin glossary.

### Auntie Eno

Raised and living in Calabar.

Eno's voice must feel rooted in household and local family life.

Her available registers:

1. **Nigerian English** — principal shared language with the Canadian-raised nephews.
2. **Nigerian Pidgin** — teasing, household command, disbelief, correction, immediate practical pressure, exchanges with local relatives.
3. **Efik** — greeting, familial intimacy, culturally specific address, moments of grief/kinship where appropriate and verified.

Eno does not need to switch languages constantly to sound Nigerian. Her cadence, syntax, social logic, directness, repetition, address terms, and code choice must distinguish her from the Vancouver brothers.

She is strongest when language feels spoken in a house, not written for a screenplay.

### Eyo and other Calabar household adults

Local Nigerian English/Pidgin code-switching should be more relaxed than Bode's with the nephews.

Among themselves, local adults may use Pidgin or Efik without translating every line when dramatic comprehension remains clear from context.

Do not create an artificial rule that every Nigerian character speaks the same amount of Pidgin.

## Code-switching functions

Use a register shift when it performs one of these actions:

- **solidarity** — local speaker aligns with another local speaker;
- **authority** — elder or household figure moves into the register that carries more social force in that relationship;
- **distance** — speaker shifts toward formal English to make a boundary clear;
- **privacy** — local family briefly excludes the diaspora brothers or children from full meaning;
- **humor** — Pidgin lands socially where formal English would feel stiff;
- **anger/urgency** — syntax compresses and may move toward the speaker's most instinctive register;
- **accommodation** — speaker returns to clearer English for the Canadian brothers;
- **grief/intimacy** — Efik or a locally rooted form may surface when emotional control weakens, subject to authenticity review.

A switch with no social function is decorative and should be cut.

## Orthography and accent rule

Do not phoneticize Nigerian English accents.

Do not write:

- arbitrary dropped consonants;
- fake misspellings;
- apostrophes to simulate pronunciation;
- exaggerated `African` syntax invented from stereotypes.

When using Nigerian Pidgin, use actual Pidgin grammar/lexicon rather than corrupted English spelling.

Because written Pidgin orthography varies, consistency within EKPO matters. Final lines require Nigerian/Calabar language review before production lock.

## Existing screenplay register — what already works

The airport porter exchange is a useful model:

```fountain
PORTER
Oga, make I help.

BODE
My brother, leave am. We no need help.

PORTER
No vex, sir.

BODE
No problem.
```

Bode changes register naturally because the social relationship changes. He does not speak that way to Maya Patel or immediately force the same register onto Malik.

The compound greetings also correctly allow Efik to establish kinship before English carries the wider conversation.

## Specific rewrite guidance — Sequences 1-7 candidate

### Sequence 4 — Airport / road

Keep the porter Pidgin exchange.

This is the clearest early proof that Bode's language shifts by audience.

On the road, Bode can remain in Nigerian English with the nephews. Do not force Pidgin into the childhood-story exchange merely for flavor.

### Sequence 5 — Homecoming

Eno's greeting register is correct in principle: Efik first, then English with the Canadian-raised nephews.

During teasing/household correction, selected English lines can become more locally idiomatic or Pidgin where it improves voice.

Candidate examples for later native review, not automatically locked wording:

- `You want argue with soup?`
- `See this one.`
- `Eat first.` / `Make you eat first.` depending register and reviewer preference.
- `You people came from Canada and forgot how to greet?` may remain because Nigerian English itself can carry the cultural voice without forced Pidgin.

Do not convert every Eno line.

### Sequence 6 — Land

Bode's land instruction should remain practical rather than occult.

Nigerian English can carry authority cleanly:

`From here, stay where I can see you.`

With Eyo/local company he may naturally use Pidgin; with the nephews he may choose English specifically so there is no ambiguity.

## Specific rewrite guidance — Sequences 8-12

### Sequence 9 — Kitchen

Current Bode/Eno dialogue is dramatically strong but linguistically too uniform in places.

Eno entering at night after hearing raised voices is a natural place for a small register shift.

Instead of converting the whole scene, allow local cadence/Pidgin at points of immediate household command while keeping essential mythology-adjacent questioning in clear English.

Example logic:

- arrival: local/immediate (`Wetin happen?`) if culturally approved;
- addressing Kai about an object brought into the house: Nigerian English or mixed register for clarity;
- ordering everybody back to bed: Pidgin/Nigerian English may carry authority naturally;
- naming Solomon's room/work: clear English because content matters more than flavor.

### Sequence 10 — Maintenance room

Keep Bode and Eno restrained and mostly clear English.

This is discovery, not a showcase for Pidgin.

Their local identity should remain audible through cadence, compression, relationship, and selective vocabulary rather than constant switching.

### Sequence 12 — Household emergency

This is the strongest future code-switch opportunity.

Under acute family danger, Eno/Eyo/local adults should not suddenly sound like North American emergency responders.

Commands to local relatives/children may naturally move into Nigerian Pidgin or Efik while instructions to Malik/Ade/Kai may return to English.

Ade's gas-leak cover should interact with this: he is a Canadian brother improvising inside a Nigerian household, while Eno actually knows how to move that household.

## Character-specific register guardrails

### Bode must never become

- generic Hollywood African elder;
- constant proverb machine;
- Pidgin-only caricature;
- secretly omniscient because his language sounds mystical.

### Eno must never become

- oracle auntie;
- exaggerated Pidgin comic relief;
- American dialogue with Nigerian food nouns pasted on top;
- translator of her own culture for the nephews/audience.

### Eyo must never become

- exposition assistant;
- generic `local cousin` voice;
- a duplicate of Bode's speech patterns.

## Translation/subtitle principle

Do not automatically parenthetically translate every Efik/Pidgin line.

If meaning is evident through action/context, allow the audience to understand socially.

If a plot-critical line requires comprehension, provide context, repetition in another register, or subtitle strategy at production stage.

## Authenticity gate

Before producer/festival/production lock, screenplay dialogue involving:

- Efik;
- extended Nigerian Pidgin;
- culturally specific Nigerian English constructions;
- age/status address forms;
- household scolding/teasing rhythms;

must receive review from an appropriate Nigerian speaker, ideally with Calabar/Efik familiarity.

AI-generated Pidgin is never the final authority.

## Final test

Cover the character names and remove cultural nouns.

Bode, Eno, Eyo, Malik, Ade, and Kai should still not sound like six people educated in the same North American writers' room.

Then ask a second question:

> Did the code-switch happen because this person needed a different register with this person at this moment?

If not, remove it.
